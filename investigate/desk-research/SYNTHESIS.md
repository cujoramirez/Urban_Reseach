# Desk-Research Synthesis

**Scope:** Sudirman corridor, Jakarta (Bundaran HI → Senayan) — pedestrian walking experience.
**Built only from sources actually collected & verified** (S1–S8 in `SOURCE-INVENTORY.md`;
exact passages in `notes/` and `sources/web-captures.md`).
**Last updated:** 2026-06-16.

> **Neutrality note (read first).** The team was flagged for confirmation bias. This synthesis
> deliberately does **not** conclude that the corridor is comfortable or uncomfortable, safe or
> unsafe. It reports what the sources establish, what they disagree on, and what they do not
> measure. Where a source rates the corridor well, that is reported as plainly as the problems.

---

## 1. Recurring themes (with sources)

1. **The main Sudirman/Thamrin axis is a relatively high-walkability, heavily-engineered
   corridor, and the revitalization measurably improved it.** Post-revitalization it scores
   "acceptable" overall on PEQI (S1) and "highly walkable" (WI 84–89) on the GWI, **up ~38.98%**
   from a 2015 baseline of ~64–71 (S9). Its trotoar was rebuilt to 8–12 m with accessibility
   features (S4), and the road diet is associated with **+15.41%** public-transport users on
   TransJakarta Corridor 1 (S9). It is the flagship of an explicit 2017–2022 revitalization
   policy (S4, S6). *(S9 is author-framed as a success — report as a measured change, not proof
   of comfort.)*
2. **Specific, named shortcomings persist even on the good corridor.** Across S1 (and echoed by
   S5's pre-2018 caveats): sidewalk space taken by **parked/through motorbikes and street
   vendors (PKL)** despite bollards; **insufficient shade**; **missing pedestrian signals/medians
   at some crossings**; **noise**; **transit-connection friction** (felt distance to Halte TJ
   GBK). These are concrete and testable in the field.
3. **Transit shapes the walking.** Both corridor studies tie pedestrian activity to transit
   nodes — segment 4's crowds form at TJ/MRT/JPO points (S1); Dukuh Atas walking frequency is
   driven by income, trip purpose, and density around the interchange (S3). Walking here is
   largely **last-mile, commuter walking**.
4. **Safety-in-public-space is a live concern at the national level, especially for women**
   (S2), and women's mobility is framed as complex daily "chains" (S8) — but neither is measured
   on this corridor.
5. **City-wide, sidewalk provision is thin** (S6: 540 km trotoar vs 6,956 km road, 2015), which
   sets up — but does not measure — the contrast with the wide Sudirman trotoar.

**What the numbers jointly suggest (neutrally):** on the *main corridor*, measured walkability
is moderate-to-good, not poor. The weaker numbers (city sidewalk coverage, fatalities, low
walk-mode share, harassment on streets) are **city/national context**, not corridor
measurements — they describe the environment the corridor sits in, not the corridor itself.

## 3. The collective QUALITATIVE picture
- The corridor "works" as a commuter spine but has recurring **comfort frictions** — shade,
  encroachment, crossings, noise, transit walking distance (S1).
- Pre-revitalization observers already flagged non-pedestrian activity, amenities, aesthetics,
  and damaged sections even while rating it highly (S5).
- Walking is chosen for being **free** and for short transit access (S3); it is routine,
  solo, peak-hour, work-oriented movement (S1, S3).

## 4. The SENTIMENT / INTENT picture
- In S1, surveyed pedestrians **prioritize comfort over safety** and **tend to tolerate/accept**
  the sidewalk "selama mampu mewadahi pergerakannya" — a key reason perception (84.7) outscores
  measured physical condition (64.3). *Tolerance is not the same as comfort* — important for an
  unbiased reading.
- Public discourse and advocacy framing centers safe, inclusive space, especially for women
  (S2, S8) and a stated government intent to "revive a walking culture" (S4). KRPA concludes
  women and gender-minority groups are **6× more vulnerable** to public-space harassment, and
  **70.56%** of those harassed said it worsened their situation (S2) — national context, but a
  reason to keep a safety/gender lens in the corridor fieldwork.

## 5. CONTRADICTIONS & tensions between sources
1. **"Acceptable scores" vs. "documented problems" (within S1).** The same study rates the
   corridor "dapat diterima" yet lists 11 concrete deficiencies. S1 itself attributes the high
   perception score partly to **method limits and user tolerance**, not necessarily to genuine
   comfort. → Do not read "74.49 / optimal perception" as proof the corridor is comfortable.
2. **Perception (84.7) vs. physical condition (64.3) (within S1).** A ~20-point gap; flagged by
   the authors as partly a measurement artifact. → Treat each track separately.
3. **Source-vs-source data error on the 2015 baseline (S5 vs S9).** Our S5 note first recorded
   the 2015 GWI as "pleasure to walk" for Sudirman & Thamrin — but that came from an **unreliable
   WebFetch summary**, and "pleasure to walk" is not a category on this GWI scale. S9 (peer-
   reviewed, citing the same Ridhani 2015 work with numbers) gives **Sudirman ≈64 "waiting to
   walk", Thamrin ≈71 "walkable."** → **S9's figures are authoritative; S5 note corrected.** A
   reminder that even a "found" source must be checked against the primary.
4. **Cross-method, cross-year comparison (S1 PEQI 2024 vs S9 GWI 2018 vs S5 GWI 2015).** Methods
   (PEQI vs GWI), segments, and years differ; S9 shows improvement 2015→2018 on GWI, while S1
   (2024, PEQI, segment 4) still logs concrete deficiencies. → A "got better then plateaued with
   residual problems" reading is *plausible* but **not provable** from these non-aligned studies;
   state it as a hypothesis for fieldwork, not a finding.
5. **Low walk-mode share <2% (S7) vs. dense peak pedestrian flows on the corridor (S1).** Not a
   true contradiction: corridor walking is concentrated **last-mile/transit** walking, invisible
   in metro-wide *primary-mode* statistics. Note as nuance.
6. **Wide, well-rated main corridor (S1, S4, S9) vs. thin city-wide sidewalk provision (S6).**
   Consistent, but they measure different things — the corridor is the exception, not the rule.

## 6. What the literature does NOT cover (the gap the fieldwork fills)
- **The "gradient" / back-streets (G1).** No collected source measures the sidewalks one block
  behind Sudirman. Every corridor study (S1, S3, S5) looks at the **main axis or a transit
  node** — never the degraded back-streets. The wide-vs-thin contrast is *implied* by S6's
  city-wide ratio but **never measured at street level on this corridor**. **This is the central
  empirical gap the team's fieldwork is positioned to fill.**
- **Corridor-specific safety/harassment.** S2 is national; there is no Sudirman-area breakdown
  (G8).
- **What "comfortable" means to *these* pedestrians.** Sources score predefined parameters;
  none open-endedly asks corridor walkers to define comfort — matching the team's added
  comfort-factor question.
- **The obstacles behind facility quality.** Policy sources (S4, S6) describe *what* was built,
  not *what blocked* better pedestrian facilities — exactly the obstacle-oriented purpose of the
  planned expert interview.
- **Day vs. night, and non-commuter users.** Corridor data skews to weekday commuters in
  daytime peaks (S1, S3); evenings, weekends, and non-worker users are uncovered.

---

### One-line takeaway (neutral)
The literature shows a **well-engineered main corridor with specific, named comfort frictions
and largely positive-to-moderate walkability scores**, sitting inside a **city with thin
sidewalk provision and real public-space safety concerns** — while saying **almost nothing about
the back-streets, about comfort in pedestrians' own words, or about why better facilities were
hard to deliver.** Those silences define the fieldwork.
