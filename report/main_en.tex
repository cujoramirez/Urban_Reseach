\begin{abstract}
Walking is the most basic form of urban mobility, yet in Jakarta it accounts for under two
percent of commute trips and sits within a city where sidewalks cover a small share of the road
network. The Jalan Jenderal Sudirman corridor is a useful case: a 2017--2022 sidewalk
revitalization pilot with a sharp contrast between a wide main footway and the near-absent
sidewalks one block behind it. This paper presents the research design for a comfort-focused
walkability study of that corridor and its back-streets. We combine a standards-referenced
segment audit, time-windowed pedestrian counts, neutral street intercepts, and an
obstacle-oriented expert interview with a computational layer: computer-vision mapping of the
sidewalk network and streetscape features, a shade and thermal-comfort layer, and interpretable
machine learning for route and mode choice. Weather is logged per observation window from a
single source to test whether conditions change comfort and behavior. The core design is a
paired observation of the main corridor against its back-street to measure the quality gradient
that prior literature leaves unmeasured. We report the instruments, data sources, benchmarks,
and cross-validation strategy; field results are deliberately withheld until data collection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Population mobility in Jakarta depends on mass transit (Transjakarta, Commuter Line, MRT), yet
the first and last leg of most trips is made on foot, by motorcycle taxi (ojek/ojol), or by
private vehicle. Walking holds a small share of commute modes: under two percent of trips in the
Greater Jakarta commuter survey are made on foot or by bicycle, with private vehicles dominant
\autocite{goodstats2024}. City-wide sidewalk provision is thin: about 540~km of sidewalk against
6{,}956~km of road in 2015 \autocite{itdp2019}. Walking in a transit-dense corridor is therefore
best understood as a last-mile connector rather than a primary mode.

The Jalan Jenderal Sudirman corridor is a strong case. It was a flagship of the 2017--2022
sidewalk revitalization program \autocite{beritajakarta2019,itdp2019}, and it shows a sharp
contrast between a wide, engineered main footway and the degraded streets one block behind it.

The essential question framing the project is: what determines whether walking in the city feels
comfortable, safe, and dignified, and for whom is that experience realized? The working challenge
statement, kept temporary and to be refined after fieldwork, is to improve the comfort of the
walking experience in the Sudirman transit corridor. The word ``comfort'' is treated as an
assumption to be tested, not a conclusion.

We formulate neutral, two-sided research questions: (1) how do pedestrians move through the
Sudirman area; (2) do pedestrians use the available sidewalks, and what influences this;
(3) what makes walking here feel comfortable or uncomfortable; (4) how do weather and shade
affect when, where, and how people walk, and how do they cope; (5) how do pedestrians know, or
fail to know, route conditions before walking; (6) how does route choice differ by time of day
and gender; and (7) what obstacles blocked the delivery of comfortable pedestrian facilities
in 2017--2022 (for the expert interview).

The scope is locked to the Sudirman corridor and its immediate back-streets, named by street
rather than by district or a list of stations. The central object of study is the quality
gradient between the wide main footway and the near-absent sidewalks behind it. The team was
flagged for confirmation bias, so the questions are kept neutral, assumptions are made explicit
and separate, and findings are withheld until field data is collected.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Walkability Concepts and Measurement}
Pedestrian-path quality is commonly scored with structured indices. The Pedestrian Environmental
Quality Index (PEQI) rates completeness, safety, and comfort from physical condition and
perception \autocite{dwisadana2024}; the Global Walkability Index (GWI) uses nine parameters
\autocite{ridhani2015,mulyadi2022}, with categories of highly walkable (index $\geq 70$), waiting
to walk ($50$--$70$), and not walkable ($\leq 50$) \autocite{mulyadi2022}. At the policy level,
design guidance derives from a Complete Streets framework \autocite{itdp2019}.

\subsection{Corridor Condition and User Satisfaction}
On the southern segment of the corridor, a post-revitalization PEQI study scored physical
condition $64.28$ and perception $84.70$, for a combined $74.49$ classified as acceptable, while
listing concrete shortfalls: motorcycles and vendors on the footway despite bollards, limited
shade, missing pedestrian signals at some crossings, and a transit distance felt as far
\autocite{dwisadana2024}. A road-diet revitalization raised GWI walkability by up to
$38.98\%$ and was associated with a $15.41\%$ rise in public-transport users, from a 2015
baseline near $64$ on Sudirman and $71$ on Thamrin \autocite{mulyadi2022,ridhani2015}. A
user-satisfaction study on the same corridor evaluated paths, street furniture, and signage from
the pedestrian side \autocite{apritasari2020satisfaction}.

\subsection{Transit and Last-Mile Walking}
Pedestrian activity concentrates at transit nodes \autocite{dwisadana2024}; at Dukuh Atas,
walking frequency is driven by income, trip purpose, and settlement density
\autocite{rakhmatulloh2020}, with a recent study reassessing walkability on a 400~m core radius
\autocite{napitupulu2025tod}. For the MRT corridor, a stated-preference discrete-choice study
reports Jakarta-specific acceptable walking distances of about $629$~m for men and $593$~m for
women, and $689$~m near offices versus $547$~m near residences \autocite{afkara2020walking}.
Field reporting documents last-mile friction near a Sudirman station, with commuters crowding the
kerb for ojol while motorcycles line the footway \autocite{kompas2025ojol}.

\subsection{Safety, Gender, and Microclimate}
Public streets are the most common offline site of harassment nationally, and women are reported
far more vulnerable \autocite{krpa2022}; women's daily mobility is framed as linked trip chains
\autocite{udk_mobilitasperempuan}. National crash data record $10{,}428$ pedestrian victims in
2023, with $54.84\%$ linked to crossing at undesignated places \autocite{korlantas2023}. Heat
shapes comfort: Sky View Factor relates to pedestrian thermal comfort on a Jakarta footway
\autocite{arif2021skyview}, and shade is a recurring shortfall in corridor audits
\autocite{dwisadana2024}.

\subsection{Informal Use, Policy, and Standards}
Pedestrian space is occupied informally; Jakarta had 324 pedestrian bridges in 2015, mostly near
transit, with leftover space colonized for unplanned uses \autocite{putri2020residual}. A 2018
policy reserved the Sudirman--Thamrin footway for pedestrians and planned to relocate vendors to
side streets behind office buildings \autocite{merdeka2018pkl}, a plausible driver of the
gradient. National technical standards set a minimum footway width of $1.85$~m, crossing spacing
of $100$--$200$~m, a maximum ramp slope of $8\%$, and a protected-crossing threshold above $450$
pedestrians per hour per effective meter \autocite{binamarga2023}.

\subsection{Computational Methods}
Recent work measures walkability from imagery with deep learning, segmenting street-view
panoramas into elements that become walkability features \autocite{walkabilitydl2024}, with
crowdsourced accessibility labeling schemes for sidewalks \autocite{projectsidewalk2019}.
Open-source semantic segmentation of aerial tiles maps sidewalk, crosswalk, and footpath into a
routable network, allowing sidewalk presence and continuity to be measured at scale
\autocite{tile2net2023}. Shade-aware routing models a sun-avoidance preference and a
CoolWalkability metric from building footprints, heights, and sun position \autocite{coolwalks2025}.
Interpretable machine learning, using gradient boosting with SHAP, extends discrete-choice models
of pedestrian route choice \autocite{routechoiceml2024,afkara2020walking}.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Research Design}
We use a convergent mixed-methods design combining three angles of evidence: objective
(standards-referenced audit, counts, sensors, computer vision), observed (pedestrian behavior),
and stated (interviews and validated perception scales). The core is a paired observation of a
main-corridor segment against its back-street to quantify the gradient.

\subsection{Scope and Units}
The unit of analysis is a segment (one block per side, named by street). Each main-corridor
segment is paired with the back-street block behind it, observed on matched days and times.
Windows span the morning peak (06:00--09:00), midday heat (11:00--14:00), evening peak
(15:00--18:30), after dark, and weekend/Car Free Day. Priority nodes are Dukuh Atas/Sudirman
station, GBK--FX, and Ratu Plaza/Menara Mandiri.

\subsection{Instruments}
The instruments are a segment audit scored 0--2 with GPS and photo references; a tally sheet for
5-minute counts (sidewalk versus road, direction to or from transit, apparent group and gender),
an obstruction inventory, and a weather and coping block; a $\leq 5$-minute consent-first
intercept script; a neutral per-persona interview guide with an obstacle-oriented expert
interview; and validated perception scales (a seven-point thermal sensation vote, a perceived
safety and comfort scale, and selected Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale items).

\subsection{Sampling}
Sampling is non-probability with quotas crossing persona, location, and time. Personas include
office commuters, women pedestrians (oversampled), students, transit last-mile users, informal
actors (vendors and ojol drivers), back-street residents, and people with reduced mobility.
Recruitment occurs at natural dwell points. The convenience nature of the sample is stated;
representativeness is not claimed.

\subsection{Data Sources}
Weather is logged per window from Apple WeatherKit (temperature, apparent temperature, humidity,
precipitation probability, UV, and condition) to keep the research and any future application on
one source, with Open-Meteo as a historical fallback and the national meteorological agency
(BMKG) as an official cross-check; attribution is shown on outputs. Other sources are air quality;
OpenStreetMap geometry with building footprints and heights; Google Street View and aerial tiles
for computer vision; a sensing walk logging temperature, humidity, and illuminance with GPS; and
geotagged review and social-media mining for public discourse.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
Audit parameters reference the national standard \autocite{binamarga2023}: minimum footway width
$1.85$~m, crossing spacing $100$--$200$~m, maximum ramp slope $8\%$, minimum overpass clearance
$5.1$~m, and the pedestrian-flow unit of persons per meter per minute. Walkability uses PEQI and
GWI \autocite{dwisadana2024,mulyadi2022}; acceptable walking distance uses Jakarta-specific values
\autocite{afkara2020walking}; safety context uses national crash data \autocite{korlantas2023};
thermal comfort references Sky View Factor and a Temperature-Humidity index
\autocite{arif2021skyview}.

\subsection{Computational Methods}
We apply the team's computer-vision and machine-learning skills to widen coverage and
objectivity. Automated sidewalk mapping segments aerial tiles into a routable network to measure
the gradient at scale \autocite{tile2net2023}; street-view segmentation yields per-point
streetscape features \autocite{walkabilitydl2024} with an accessibility label scheme
\autocite{projectsidewalk2019}; a shade layer follows the CoolWalks approach from building
footprints, heights, and sun position \autocite{coolwalks2025}; and an interpretable model
(gradient boosting with SHAP) extends the discrete-choice baseline
\autocite{afkara2020walking,routechoiceml2024}. Models trained on other cities require validation
against the Jakarta field audit, and each model is treated as a hypothesis generator rather than
proof.

\subsection{Cross-Validation and Ethics}
Each claim is tested from the three angles. Computer-vision outputs are ground-truthed against the
field audit, sensor readings are compared with WeatherKit, and audits are scored by multiple
raters for reliability. Agreement strengthens a finding; disagreement is reported as insight. Every
intercept begins with verbal consent, with separate consent for recording; sensitive and after-dark
intercepts are led by a female team member with a buddy. Faces in photographs are blurred, and
service terms are respected (WeatherKit attribution, Google terms, OpenStreetMap license).

\section{Expected Results and Discussion}
Field results are withheld until data collection (target after the field week). The analysis will
report the measured gradient between the main corridor and its back-streets, the relationship
between weather and observed comfort behavior, and the agreement between computer-vision estimates
and the field audit. No conclusion about corridor comfort or safety is drawn in advance.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper sets out a neutral, standards-referenced, and computationally extended research design
for a comfort-focused walkability study of the Sudirman corridor. The contribution at this stage
is the design itself: a paired-gradient measurement, a weather-joined observation protocol, and a
computer-vision and machine-learning pipeline validated against field ground truth. Findings will
follow the fieldwork.
